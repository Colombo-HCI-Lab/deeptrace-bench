## Models

### Audio (10)

<a id="model-aasist"></a>

#### AASIST

AASIST (Jung et al., ICASSP 2022) listens to the raw waveform and builds graphs over the frequency bands and over the time steps, then uses graph attention to weigh which parts of the signal look artificial. Speech made by synthesis often leaves small traces in the spectrum or in the timing, and the authors designed one network that catches both without needing an ensemble. The released weights were trained only on the ASVspoof 2019 LA training split. It is a compact standard baseline: scored on the full ASVspoof 2019 LA eval set here it reaches an equal error rate (EER) of 1.58%, against 0.83% in the paper, but on In-the-Wild, which it never saw, it sits close to chance (EER 44.0%).

<a id="model-aasist_l"></a>

#### AASIST-L

AASIST-L is the lightweight variant of AASIST from the same paper (Jung et al., ICASSP 2022). It uses the same graph idea on the raw waveform with far fewer parameters, which the authors put at about 85 thousand, so it is cheap to run. Its released weights were trained only on the ASVspoof 2019 LA training split. Scored on the full ASVspoof 2019 LA eval set here it reaches an EER of 1.49% (paper 0.99%), and on In-the-Wild it is near chance (EER 44.9%), so it shows how little a small model trained on one corpus carries over to other data.

<a id="model-aasist3"></a>

#### AASIST3

AASIST3 (Borodin et al., 2024) was built for the ASVspoof 2024 challenge. It extends AASIST by feeding in features from a pretrained speech model (wav2vec 2.0 XLSR-53) and by swapping some ordinary layers for Kolmogorov-Arnold network (KAN) layers, a newer kind of learned layer. The released weights saw ASVspoof 2019 LA, ASVspoof 5, MLAAD and M-AILABS after the front end was pretrained on Common Voice, BABEL and Multilingual LibriSpeech, so the benchmark refuses it on any MLAAD-based evalset. The repository says these weights are not the ones behind the paper's numbers, and the Speech DF Arena measures them at 28 to 32% EER on In-the-Wild and ASVspoof 2021, so it is kept here as a weak multilingual baseline, not a strong one.

<a id="model-antideepfake_mms_300m"></a>

#### AntiDeepfake MMS-300M

AntiDeepfake MMS-300M comes from the NII Yamagishi Lab (Ge et al., 2025, "Post-training for Deepfake Speech Detection"). The authors take a speech model that was pretrained on ordinary speech and give it a middle "post-training" stage on a large mix of real and fake speech, so the model learns about artefacts before any task-specific tuning. This one starts from MMS-300M, which was pretrained on about 1,400 languages, and is post-trained on 74 thousand hours from 29 corpora in over 100 languages, including FLEURS, MLAAD, all of ASVspoof 2019 LA, LJSpeech and VoxCeleb2. That wide exposure makes it a strong multilingual candidate for South Asian audio, but the benchmark refuses it on MLAAD-based evalsets and on anything built from FLEURS. The model card reports an In-the-Wild EER of 2.90%.

<a id="model-antideepfake_w2v_small"></a>

#### AntiDeepfake wav2vec 2.0 small

AntiDeepfake wav2vec 2.0 small is the smallest model of the NII Yamagishi Lab's AntiDeepfake series (Ge et al., 2025). It is a wav2vec 2.0 base model (95 million parameters) pretrained on English LibriSpeech only, then post-trained on the same 74 thousand hours of real and fake speech from 29 corpora as the MMS-300M model. It decides real versus fake by pooling its speech features over time and passing them to one linear layer. Because the post-training data are identical, comparing it with the MMS-300M version isolates the effect of multilingual pretraining. The model card reports an In-the-Wild EER of 4.24%, against 2.90% for MMS-300M, and the benchmark refuses it on MLAAD-based evalsets and on anything built from FLEURS.

<a id="model-df_arena_1b"></a>

#### DF Arena 1B

DF Arena 1B is a universal speech anti-spoofing model released by the Speech DF Arena project (Speech-Arena-2025, 2025), the team behind a public leaderboard for audio deepfake detectors. It runs the audio through the large XLS-R 1B speech model, pools the features of every layer with learned attention weights, and passes them to a conformer, a transformer-style block with convolutions, that makes the call. Its XLS-R front end was pretrained on Common Voice, BABEL, MLS, VoxPopuli and VoxLingua107, and it was then trained on ASVspoof 2019 and 2024, MLAAD, Codecfake, LibriSeVoc, DFADD, CtrSVDD, SpoofCeleb and EnvSDD, so the benchmark refuses it on ASVspoof 2019 LA eval and MLAAD. It is the top open model on that leaderboard, and here it reproduces the model card's In-the-Wild result (EER 0.67% on the full set, against the card's 0.91%).

#### XLS-R 300M + AASIST

XLS-R + AASIST (Tak et al., Odyssey 2022) puts the AASIST graph network on top of wav2vec 2.0-style speech features, here from the XLS-R 300M model, and fine-tunes the whole thing on spoofing data. The front end was pretrained on real speech only (Common Voice, BABEL, MLS, VoxPopuli and VoxLingua107), and the full network was then trained on the ASVspoof 2019 LA training split alone. The authors report the lowest equal error rates published at the time on ASVspoof 2021 LA and DF. Training on one corpus makes it a clean zero-shot probe on South Asian sets, with no MLAAD or FLEURS in its training. Its adapter replaces the original fairseq code, so parity with upstream is not checked yet.

#### XLS-R 300M + SLS

XLS-R + SLS (Zhang et al., ACM Multimedia 2024) is built on the observation that different layers of the XLS-R speech model hold different spoofing clues. Its Sensitive Layer Selection (SLS) module pools the outputs of all 24 layers with learned attention weights before a small classifier makes the call. The front end was pretrained on real speech (Common Voice, BABEL, MLS, VoxPopuli and VoxLingua107), and the network was trained on the ASVspoof 2019 LA training split alone. It is the strongest open model trained on that corpus only, so it works as a clean zero-shot probe on every South Asian set. The repository declares no licence, so its code is imported but never copied, and its parity with upstream is not checked yet.

#### XLSR-Mamba

XLSR-Mamba (Xiao and Das, IEEE SPL 2024) replaces the attention layers of the back end with Mamba, a state space model that reads a sequence efficiently, and runs it forwards and backwards over XLS-R speech features. The front end was pretrained on real speech (Common Voice, BABEL, MLS, VoxPopuli and VoxLingua107), and the released network was trained on the ASVspoof 2019 LA training split alone. The authors report that on In-the-Wild it emerged as the strongest candidate among the systems they compared. Here it runs without the original compiled Mamba kernels, using plain PyTorch stand-ins that are slower, and parity against those kernels is not checked yet.

### Video (15)

<a id="model-altfreezing"></a>

#### AltFreezing

AltFreezing (Wang et al., CVPR 2023) looks at short face video clips with a 3D convolutional network, so it can pick up both visual flaws in single frames and unnatural motion between frames. Such a network tends to lean on one kind of clue, so during training the authors alternately freeze its space-related weights and its time-related weights, which forces it to learn both. The released weights were trained on FaceForensics++ (compression level not stated). It is built for generalising to unseen manipulations, but it is blocked in this benchmark: the weights are only on RecDrive and Baidu, upstream issues report the file gone, and there is no adapter yet.

<a id="model-core"></a>

#### CORE

CORE (Ni et al., CVPRW 2022) is an Xception image network, run here from DeepfakeBench's release, that checks individual face frames. During training it sees the same face under different augmentations, such as crops and colour changes, and a loss term pushes its internal representations of those views to stay close, which the authors say makes them more consistent and relieves overfitting. At test time it is used like a plain Xception classifier, scoring each frame. The released weights were trained on FaceForensics++ (c23 compression per the DeepfakeBench README), after ImageNet pretraining. It adds one training idea on top of the Xception baseline, so it helps show whether such tricks carry over to South Asian faces.

#### EfficientNet-B4

EfficientNet-B4 is a standard image network, here DeepfakeBench's single-network version of the detector studied by Bonettini et al. (ICPR 2020), whose paper combines several EfficientNet-B4 variants into an ensemble. It looks at one cropped face frame at a time and learns the visual artefacts of manipulation from examples. The released weights were trained on FaceForensics++ (c23 compression per the DeepfakeBench README), after ImageNet pretraining. It is a classic image-only baseline for video, and together with Xception it shows how far a plain CNN gets before any special forgery-detection ideas are added.

<a id="model-effort"></a>

#### Effort

Effort (Yan et al., ICML 2025) adapts a large pretrained CLIP vision model by splitting its weight matrices with singular value decomposition, freezing the main components that hold general knowledge, and training only the small remainder to spot fakes. The authors argue that ordinary fine-tuning overfits to a few fake patterns, and that this split keeps the model's feature space rich enough to generalise. The paper is about AI-generated images in general, and here DeepfakeBench's Effort detector scores face frames. The released weights were trained on FaceForensics++ (c23) after CLIP pretraining on OpenAI's private data. It is blocked for now because Google Drive refused the weight file as over quota on 2026-10-05, and upstream reports a reproduction gap (Celeb-DF v2 AUC 0.885 against 0.956) and publishes no licence file.

<a id="model-f3net"></a>

#### F3Net

F3Net (Qian et al., ECCV 2020) detects fake faces by looking at frequency, not only at pixels. It splits each face image into bands of fine and coarse detail with a discrete cosine transform and feeds the bands to an Xception network, because manipulation can leave traces in those bands that the eye misses, especially after video compression. The released DeepfakeBench weights were trained on FaceForensics++ (c23) after ImageNet pretraining. The detector's yaml names only NeuralTextures, which may not describe the checkpoint, and the benchmark treats it as trained on all of FF++. It is the frequency-cue member of the classic video group.

#### FFD

FFD (Dang et al., CVPR 2020) is an Xception network with an attention map. The map highlights which parts of the face look manipulated and steers the classifier towards them, and it can also be shown to a person as a rough picture of where the forgery is. The released DeepfakeBench weights were trained on FaceForensics++ (c23 per the DeepfakeBench README), after ImageNet pretraining. It is one of the classic image-based detectors in the benchmark, here representing the idea of focusing on the manipulated region, and its scores match the original code closely in the parity check.

<a id="model-gend"></a>

#### GenD (CLIP ViT-L/14)

GenD (Yermakov et al., WACV 2026) takes a large pretrained CLIP vision model and tunes only its LayerNorm parameters, about 0.03% of the model, so the general knowledge in CLIP stays mostly intact. It also forces the face features onto a sphere with L2 normalisation and metric learning, and each frame gets a real-or-fake probability that is averaged over the video. The released weights were trained on FaceForensics++ only, after CLIP pretraining on OpenAI's private image-text data. The authors report that it beats more complex methods in average cross-dataset AUROC over 14 benchmarks, which makes it the main generalisation-first video model here. Its adapter runs end to end, but parity with upstream is not yet checked.

<a id="model-gend_pe_l"></a>

#### GenD (Perception Encoder L/14)

This is GenD's second released backbone: the same recipe as GenD (Yermakov et al., WACV 2026), built on Meta's Perception Encoder instead of CLIP. Only the LayerNorm parameters are tuned, the face features are L2-normalised, and a video's score is the mean over its frames. The released weights were trained on FaceForensics++ only, after Meta's pretraining of the encoder on data that is not public, so overlap with our data cannot be checked. Because it differs from the CLIP version only in backbone, comparing the two shows how much the pretrained foundation model matters. Its adapter has not yet been through the parity check.

<a id="model-lipforensics"></a>

#### LipForensics

LipForensics (Haliassos et al., CVPR 2021) watches only the mouth. A network is first taught to lipread, so it learns what natural mouth movement looks like, and its temporal part is then fine-tuned to tell real from forged mouth motion. The authors argue this high-level cue holds up across different manipulations and after compression, because it does not depend on low-level artefacts. The released weights were pretrained on LRW and fine-tuned on FaceForensics++ (c23, all four manipulations). It matters here because lip-sync fakes are a focus of this benchmark, though users report that its FF++ numbers do not reproduce, and it needs at least 25 consecutive frames with a detectable face.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| How it works | 25-frame clips of 96 px grayscale mouth crops (88 px centre crop), ResNet-18 with 3D stem, multi-scale temporal convolutional network, one logit per clip |
| Input | mouth crops from 25 consecutive frames at a time (up to 100 frames per video) |
| Paper | [Lips Don't Lie: A Generalisable and Robust Approach to Face Forgery Detection](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.07657), CVPR 2021 · 663 citations |
| Training | pretrained on LRW; trained on FaceForensics++ (train split) |
| Size | 36 M parameters |
| Cost | 17.8 GFLOPs and 5.7 ms per mouth clip on a 24 GB GPU (fp32) |
| Licence | MIT (FAN landmarks from face-alignment, BSD-3) |

<a id="model-recce"></a>

#### RECCE

RECCE (Cao et al., CVPR 2022) learns what real faces look like by training a decoder to rebuild them from the encoder's features. Fake faces rebuild poorly, and the reconstruction differences are used to guide the classifier, a cue that does not depend on any one manipulation method. The released DeepfakeBench weights were trained on FaceForensics++ (c23 per the DeepfakeBench README), after ImageNet pretraining. It is the reconstruction-based detector in the classic video group, and its scores match the original code closely in the parity check.

<a id="model-sbi"></a>

#### SBI

SBI (Shiohara and Yamasaki, CVPR 2022) is trained on fakes it makes itself. Each real face is blended with a slightly altered copy of itself, which produces the seams and statistical mismatches that face swaps leave, without using any real deepfake method. The authors argue this makes the network learn general forgery traces instead of the style of one generator. Only the real videos of FaceForensics++ were used, after ImageNet pretraining, and the c23 checkpoint is the one used here. It is a widely cited baseline for generalisation, and its research-only licence means its code is never copied into this repository.

<a id="model-spsl"></a>

#### SPSL

SPSL (Liu et al., CVPR 2021) adds the phase of the image's frequency spectrum as an extra input channel next to the colour image. The authors observe that the upsampling steps in most face forgery methods change that phase in a visible way, so it carries a clue that colour alone hides. The released DeepfakeBench weights were trained on FaceForensics++ (c23) after ImageNet pretraining. The detector's yaml lists only FaceSwap, which may not describe the checkpoint, and the benchmark treats it as trained on all of FF++. It is the phase-cue member of the classic video group, and it uses the full Xception network, not the shallower one of the paper.

| Training | pretrained on ImageNet-1k; trained on FaceForensics++ (train split) |
| Size | 22 M parameters |
| Cost | 11.9 GFLOPs and 1.7 ms per frame on a 24 GB GPU (fp32) |

<a id="model-srm"></a>

#### SRM

SRM (Luo et al., CVPR 2021) argues that ordinary detectors overfit to colour textures that are specific to one forgery method. It therefore adds a second stream that keeps only the fine, high-frequency noise of the image, where tampered and genuine regions tend to differ, and lets the two streams guide each other with attention. The released DeepfakeBench weights were trained on FaceForensics++ (c23 per the DeepfakeBench README), after ImageNet pretraining. It is the noise-residual cue in the classic video group, and its scores match the original code closely in the parity check.

| Training | pretrained on ImageNet-1k; trained on FaceForensics++ (train split) |
| Size | 55 M parameters |

<a id="model-ucf"></a>

#### UCF

UCF (Yan et al., ICCV 2023) tries to generalise to forgery methods it has not seen. It splits what it sees in a face into three parts: features unrelated to forgery, features specific to one manipulation method, and features common to all forgeries. Only the common features are used for the real-or-fake decision, so the score does not depend on the quirks of a particular generator. The released DeepfakeBench weights were trained on all four FaceForensics++ methods (c23) after ImageNet pretraining. It is the disentangling approach in the classic video group, and its scores match the original code closely in the parity check.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| How it works | Aligned 256 px face crop, Xception encoders separating content, method-specific forgery and common forgery features, scored with the common-forgery head only |
| Input | 256 px aligned face crops, 32 frames per video |
| Paper | [UCF: Uncovering Common Features for Generalizable Deepfake Detection](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13949), ICCV 2023 · 338 citations |
| Training | pretrained on ImageNet-1k; trained on FaceForensics++ (train split) |
| Size | 47 M parameters |
| Cost | 24.1 GFLOPs and 4.2 ms per frame on a 24 GB GPU (fp32) |
| Licence | CC BY-NC 4.0 (DeepfakeBench) |
| Status here | adapter written, parity checked |

<a id="model-xception"></a>

#### Xception

Xception is a standard image network, and this is the face-forgery detector built on it in the FaceForensics++ paper (Rossler et al., ICCV 2019). It looks at one cropped face frame at a time and learns the visual artefacts of manipulation from examples. The released DeepfakeBench weights were trained on FaceForensics++ (c23 per the README, though upstream issue 151 asking whether every checkpoint is c23 is unanswered), after ImageNet pretraining. It is the standard baseline for video deepfake detection and the model the other DeepfakeBench detectors build on. Some users report that DeepfakeBench's released weights do not reproduce its README table.

### Audio-visual (1)

<a id="model-havic"></a>

#### HAVIC

HAVIC (CVPR 2026 Findings) is an audio-visual detector that asks whether the sound and the moving face in a video belong together. It first learns what coherent audio, video and cross-modal timing look like by pretraining on real videos only, then combines the audio and visual features to score a clip, which should help against fakes whose lips, voice or expression disagree. Its weights were pretrained on LRS2 and fine-tuned on a random 70% of FakeAVCeleb, and because that split's item list is unpublished the benchmark refuses it on FakeAVCeleb. It is the only open audio-visual detector here, and it needs videos of at least 3.2 seconds with a face in 16 sampled frames, so HiDF's 3-second clips are too short.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| How it works | 16-frame windows of 224 px face frames plus 128-bin audio filterbank, audio and video transformer encoders pretrained on real videos, audio-visual interaction module, MLP classifier, one logit per window |
| Input | native-size face-box crops at 5 frames per second (up to 50), with the audio under them |
| Paper | [Leave No Stone Unturned: Uncovering Holistic Audio-Visual Intrinsic Coherence for Deepfake Detection](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23960), CVPR 2026 Findings · 1 citations |
| Training | pretrained on LRS2; fine-tuned on FakeAVCeleb |
| Size | 215 M parameters |
| Cost | 536.4 GFLOPs and 46.4 ms per frame on a 24 GB GPU (fp32) |
| Licence | MIT |
